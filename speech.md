## 1 · Title
**0:00** · 50s

> The only part that changes on the day. Listen to the speakers before you and name what they actually said. Never let this run past ninety seconds.

Everyone who has spoken today has been talking about the same thing under a different name. Tax efficiency. Appreciation. Square footage. A credit facility.

*(beat)*

All of it is leverage.

*(beat)*

And nobody has answered the only question that actually matters about leverage, which is how you get some.

For most of us the answer is a mortgage. And a traditional mortgage asks you for exactly two things. Show me enough income. Put down twenty to twenty-five percent.

Both of those are brakes. The income test punishes you for every dollar your CPA legally saved you this morning. The down payment ties up capital you were going to deploy somewhere else. So you end up somewhere strange: the better you get at building wealth, the harder it gets to borrow against it.

I'm Gaurav Vaish, with Doma Loans. That code saves my details, and it will be up again at the end.

## 9 · 02 Why borrow
**6:45** · 50s

> The best line in the talk for this room.

So why borrow at all? He could write the check.

*(wait. Somebody will answer.)*

Because writing the check means liquidating one point seven million dollars. Long-term gains, net investment income tax, and California on top. The mortgage is not the expensive option. The mortgage is the tax-efficient one.

> Nod toward the host.

You spent this morning learning exactly that. This is the same lesson with a deed attached.

## 11 · 02b The ratio
**8:15** · 55s

> Walk the table top to bottom.

Thirty-five thousand a month coming in. Nineteen nine hundred going out. Fifty-seven percent. Declined.

> The turn.

He is holding one point eight million in vested company stock. He cannot pledge it, and frankly you would not want him to, because a securities-backed line is one more monthly payment landing on the exact ratio that is already failing.

*(beat)*

So we do not pledge it. We do not sell it. We count it. One point eight million over a hundred and twenty months is fifteen thousand a month. Blended debt-to-income: forty percent. Approved.

He never sold a single share.

## 13 · 03 The bridge
**9:55** · 1m 5s

> Lead with the product, not the steps.

Twelve months. No interest. No payments.

*(let it sit. Somebody will ask you to say it again.)*

> Then the five steps, ten seconds each, no elaboration. Then the underwriting point, which is the part that actually matters.

No monthly payment means no monthly obligation, so the bridge never enters debt-to-income at all. And if they rent the old house instead of rushing the sale, that rental income counts toward qualifying too.

> Then stop and deliver. This is the line the realtors will repeat.

They never had a money problem. They had a sequencing problem.

## 15 · 04 What it takes
**11:50** · 50s

> Read across the strip, left to right, slowing right down on the last two cells.

Two to twelve months of cash reserves. Up to three million dollars.

> Point.

Income documented: none. Employment verified: none.

*(let it sit. It is the most surprising thing you will say all afternoon.)*

> Defuse it before anybody in the room holding a license gets nervous.

That is not an absence of underwriting. The reserves get verified. The credit gets pulled. The down payment gets sourced. We just stop pretending a tax return can predict an IPO.

## 18 · Common sense
**13:30** · 35s

> Do not name the objection. Most of this room has never heard the industry term for any of this, and saying it out loud only hands them a word to be nervous about. State the standard instead.

Every borrower I have described put real money down, carries strong credit, and has verified reserves. Nothing here was taken on faith.

> Then the definition, slowly.

A good loan measures the right thing and then proves it. That is all any of these were.

> If a lender or a realtor raises the jargon in Q&A, answer it plainly then. Never raise it yourself from the stage.
